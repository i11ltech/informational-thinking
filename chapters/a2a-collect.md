# Collect

**Principle: collect what your question needs, not what is easiest to reach. The data you need is often already in front of you, unnoticed, so collecting starts with looking on purpose.**



## Seeing is not observing

You see everything around you on the way to work, but you rarely look for anything specific, and you seldom note anything down to use later. *Observing* is different. It means paying close, active attention so that you gather specific information.

Familiarity works against you here. The more often you pass something, the less of it you notice. This is why the first skill of informational thinking is an eye for detail: noticing which data exists, and which of it is *signal*, the part that bears on your question.

The question from [Information vs Data](#a1) is what makes observing possible. Without a question, everything is equally worth noticing, which means nothing is. With a question, you know what to look for.



## Two ways collecting goes wrong

**Collecting what is near.** The data that is easiest to reach is rarely the data the question needs. A dashboard someone already built, the last report, the first search result: each is convenient, and each answers someone else's question. Ask whether you would have chosen this data if you had to go and get it.

**Collecting everything.** The opposite mistake treats data as an asset: something you gather and hoard in case it is useful later. More data does not mean more information. It means more *noise*, data that does not bear on your question, to dig through before you find the signal.

Both mistakes have the same cause: collecting before the question is clear.



## Collect the what and the why

Data comes from three kinds of source:

* **Records:** numbers, logs, documents, anything already written down.
* **People:** the ones who do the work, use the product or feel the problem.
* **Direct observation:** going to where the thing happens and watching it.

Records tell you *what* is happening. People and observation tell you *why*. As [Product Management Thinking](#a0) argued, a number on its own is easy to misread. If everything you collected came from one kind of source, you have half the picture.



## The bridge: product discovery

Product managers have a name for the habit of collecting before deciding: *continuous discovery*. They interview customers, study product analytics, look at competitors, and ask "why" repeatedly until they reach a cause. They also *go and see*, a habit known as *genchi genbutsu* or "get out of the building". And they *instrument* products, building in measurement so that data on behaviour is recorded as it happens instead of being reconstructed later.

**Where this book departs:** product discovery is a team activity, run over weeks, about customers. Here, collecting is usually one person gathering data for one decision, often in minutes. Often what you collect is the *context* you hand to an AI tool. The same tests apply at that smaller scale. Did you pick this data because the question needs it, or because it was near? Do you have the why as well as the what?



## What to do differently

Take the question you wrote at the end of [Information vs Data](#a1).

1. List the data you would reach for first. Next to each item, mark whether you chose it because the question needs it or because it was near.
2. Check your sources. If they are all records, add one person to ask or one place to go and watch. If they are all people, find one record.
3. Cross out anything you are collecting "just in case".

To train the eye for detail itself, try this once a day for a week: look at a familiar place, such as your desk, your street or a screen you use every day, and find three things you have never noticed before. The point isn't the three things. It's learning that there was more there than you were seeing.
