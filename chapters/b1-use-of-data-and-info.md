# Use of Data and Information

**Principle: information is not used until it changes what someone does. Using it means translating it into specific actions for whoever will act, and keeping the data behind it within reach, because the work will keep needing it.**



## Knowing is not using

Most people have sat in a class or a meeting and wondered, "When will I ever use this?" The question is fair. Information you understand but never act on has cost you the effort of getting it and returned nothing.

Information literacy describes the last ability as *applying* information: using it to complete a task, form an opinion, solve a problem, take a position or create something new. Each of those is a change in what someone does. That is the test for this chapter. If nothing changed, the information was received but not used.

The space between the two has a name: the *knowing-doing gap*. Section A was about crossing from data to information. Section B starts by crossing this second gap, from information to action.



## Translate it into actions

A *decision* is a choice that commits you to an action. The information you structured in [Structure](#a2e) supports a decision, but it doesn't yet say who does what.

In [the restaurant](#metaphor), the menu is information, and nobody eats a menu. The kitchen turns it into recipes, a shopping list and a prep list for each cook. Each of those is the same information rewritten as something a particular person can act on.

Do the same with yours. For each thing the information tells you, write the action it leads to: **who** does **what**, by **when**. Different people need different actions from the same information, just as they needed different structures for it. When the one acting is an AI tool, the action is the instruction you give it.



## Make it yours before you use it

Information that came from somewhere else, such as a report, a source or an AI answer, often sounds right and still doesn't fit your situation. Before you act on it, relate it to your own case:

* **State the facts** it rests on.
* **Name the rule or principle** it implies.
* **Ask questions:** how does this apply here, and what is different about my situation?
* **Make a judgment:** compare it with what you already know.
* **Give an example** of it in your own situation.

The last one is the real test. If you can't give an example, you don't yet understand the information well enough to use it.



## Keep the data within reach

Using information keeps calling on the data underneath it. The head count from the restaurant's first conversation sets the quantities on the shopping list, long after the menu was decided. If the data stays behind in Section A, in a file nobody opens again, the people acting have to guess.

So carry forward what the actions depend on: the key facts, the *known limits* you wrote in [Process](#a2b), and where the rest can be found.



## The bridge: handoffs

Project management uses *systems and dependency thinking* to map how one team's output becomes another team's input. Project managers look for these *handoffs* early, because a small delay or misunderstanding at a handoff spreads through everything that depends on it.

Using information is a handoff: the output of Section A becomes the input of Section B.

**Where this book departs:** in a team, handoffs happen between departments, and someone owns each one. Here the handoff is often between you as the person who thought it through and you as the person who acts, or between you and an AI tool. Nobody owns it, so it is easy for something to get lost. The reasoning, the limits and the "why" are what usually drop out. The fix is the same as in a team: make the handoff explicit, and write down what the next step needs.



## What to do differently

Take the information you structured in [Structure](#a2e).

1. Write each action it leads to as **who, what, by when**.
2. Next to each action, write the one fact it depends on.
3. Give one example of the information at work in your own situation. If you can't, go back to [Interpret](#a2d).
4. Pick the smallest action you can take today, and take it. [Plan and prepare](#b2) covers the rest.
