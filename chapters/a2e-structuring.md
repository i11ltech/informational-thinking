# Structure

**Principle: information decides nothing until it reaches the person who decides, in a form they can use. Structure it for that reader and that decision: the answer first, then what supports it, then the detail.**



## Right but buried

You can collect the right data, check it, organize it and read it correctly, and still fail at the last step. The answer is in there somewhere: on page four, in the third chart, after the background. The reader runs out of time or attention before they reach it, and decides without it.

*Structuring* is presenting information concisely and accessibly. That doesn't mean decorating it. It means shaping it so the reader gets what they need in the order they need it.

The reader is not always someone else. It may be a colleague, an AI tool you are giving context to, or you next week, when you have forgotten why you collected any of it. Each of them needs the same thing: to know quickly what the information means for the decision.



## Answer first

Journalism has a name for this: the **inverted pyramid**. The most important point goes at the top. Supporting details come next. Background and context come last.

Most people write in the opposite order, the order they did the work in: first what they collected, then how they checked it, then what they found. That order makes sense to the writer, but the reader has to wade through it to reach what matters to them.



## Show the essential, keep the rest within reach

Two ideas from information architecture help here.

* **Progressive disclosure:** show only what is essential at first, and put the detail one layer down, where the reader can find it if they want it. The detail isn't removed. It is just not in the way.
* **Chunking:** a long block of data is hard to take in. Break it into small, separate units, such as short lists, headed sections or one idea per line, so the reader can take them one at a time.

One piece of detail should always stay close to the answer: the **known limits** you wrote in [Process](#a2b). An answer presented without its limits looks more certain than it is.



## Show something

If people can't picture it, it is hard for them to act on it. When the pattern *is* the message, such as a trend or a gap between two groups, a simple picture often carries it faster than a paragraph. Product managers use visualizations for this reason: to break complex data into pieces that someone who wasn't in the analysis can take in.

The test for a picture is the same as for a sentence: does it make the answer to the question easier to see? A chart that shows everything you found, rather than the one thing that matters, is the buried answer again.



## The bridge: information architecture and product communication

Information architecture supplies the inverted pyramid, progressive disclosure and chunking, all aimed at reducing how much effort a reader must spend to understand. Product management adds the habit of presenting data to stakeholders in a form they can act on.

**Where this book departs:** information architecture usually designs for many readers it will never meet. You usually know your reader and the decision they face. That lets you go further: structure for *one* decision, and leave out everything that doesn't bear on it, however much work went into it.

When the reader is an AI tool, the same structure holds. Put what you want first, then the context that supports it, then the limits. A request structured this way is easier for the tool to act on, just as it is for a person.



## What to do differently

Take the five-part sentence you wrote in [Interpret](#a2d) and rebuild it for your reader:

1. **First line:** what this means for the decision. If the reader is an AI tool, what you want it to do.
2. **Next two or three lines:** the pattern, its cause and the alternative you ruled out.
3. **Last:** the known limits and what would change your mind.

Then test it. If your reader stopped after the first line, could they decide? If not, rewrite that line until they could.
