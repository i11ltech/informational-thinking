# Information vs Data

**Principle: data becomes information only when it answers a question someone needs answered to decide something. Start from the question, not from the data.**



## Data is not yet information

*Data* is recorded facts and figures, such as numbers, events and observations, taken on their own. A list of daily sales figures is data. A folder of log files is data.

*Information* is data placed in context so it answers a specific question and can support a decision. "Sales fell on the three rainy days this month" is information, but only for someone who is deciding something about rainy days.

The same data can be information to one person and noise to another. What decides it is the question, not the data. A pile of facts with no question attached has potential and nothing more.

*Knowledge* is what you bring from experience and expertise. When you add knowledge to information, you can make a stronger decision than either one would give you alone.



## Why start from the question

If you start from the data, you collect more, look at more and still don't know what you're looking for. If you start from the question, you can tell which data matters before you open it.

Questions also come in kinds, and each kind needs different data:

* **Lookup:** what is the value? (total for one month)
* **Comparison:** how does this differ from that? (this quarter against last)
* **Trend:** which way is it moving? (what is growing fastest)
* **Segmentation:** which group behaves differently? (customers who haven't bought in 90 days)
* **Anomaly:** what is unusual? (a sudden spike this week)

Once you know what kind of question you have, you know what data to look for.



## The bridge: data as a product

Product management has a name for this shift: treating **data as a product** instead of as an asset. You collect and hoard an asset. You design a product for a user, starting from what that user needs to do, and then you make it easy to find, trust and use.

Information literacy, as the American Library Association defines it, makes the same point from the reader's side. Its first ability is to "recognize when information is needed". Locating and evaluating come after that.

**Where this book departs:** data-as-a-product is written for data teams that serve other people's decisions. This book applies it to your own thinking. The user you design for is you, in a moment when you have to decide something. The "product" is the one fact, put in context, that moves that decision.



## The cost of skipping this

Russell Ackoff warned: "The righter we do the wrong thing, the wronger we become." Cleaning, charting and reporting data that answers no decision is doing the wrong thing right. Section A of this book is about choosing the right thing. Choosing the right information is where that starts.



## What to do differently

Before you collect, open or ask for any data, write two lines:

1. **The decision:** what will I decide, and by when?
2. **The question:** what do I need to know to decide it? Is it a lookup, comparison, trend, segmentation or anomaly question?

If you can't write the first line, you don't need information yet. You need a decision. If you can write both, you can judge every piece of data by one test: does it help answer this question?
