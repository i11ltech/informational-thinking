# Plan and Prepare

**Principle: a plan turns a decision into steps you can check against. Before you start, confirm what you are doing, decide what success looks like, check what you have, and begin with the smallest version that can teach you something.**



## Confirm what you are doing

In [the restaurant](#metaphor), the first step after the menu is chosen is to confirm it with the client: the dishes, the numbers, what is included and what isn't. A misunderstanding found now costs a phone call. Found during the event, it costs a course.

Write down the *scope*: what is in, and what is out. The second half matters as much as the first. Project managers watch for *silent scope creep*: requirements that grow a little at a time without anyone deciding they should. From the inside it looks like slow progress. An "out" list is what lets you notice it.



## Decide what success looks like

Define, before you start, what a successful result looks like, in terms you can check without arguing about it. [Project Management Thinking](#b0) called this deciding what *done* means. It matters even more when an AI tool does the work, because its output looks finished whether or not it is right.

Tie success to the *outcome*, what actually changes, not only the output. Then add one guardrail, as [Product Management Thinking](#a0) suggested: a second measure that warns you when the gain is costing something else. A meal served on time but cold isn't a success.



## Check what you have

List the resources the plan needs and mark which ones you actually have: people, time, money, tools, and the information itself. For an AI tool, the context you give it is a resource. If it is missing, the tool will fill the gap with a guess.

Then look at the order. Some steps can't start until others finish. The longest chain of those dependent steps is the *critical path*, and it sets the earliest you can finish. Put your attention there.



## Estimate honestly

Most estimates are wishes. Three habits from project management make them more honest:

* **Give a range,** not a single date, as [Project Management Thinking](#b0) suggested.
* **Make the buffer visible.** *Parkinson's law* observes that work expands to fill the time allotted. Padding hidden inside every task tends to get used up. Take it out of the individual tasks and keep one shared buffer at the end, where you can see it being spent.
* **Learn from your past estimates.** Compare what you planned last time with what actually happened, and adjust this estimate by the difference.



## Start small

Instead of committing everything at once, scale the plan down to a small, low-risk test first: one dish cooked for a tasting before the full event. A small test is where you find out which of your assumptions were wrong, while it is still cheap to change course.

This is also where the [pre-mortem](#b0) earns its place. The risks it surfaced are what your first small test should check.



## The bridge: project planning

Project planning exists to coordinate work: who does what, in what order, with what resources, by when. Its tools, including scope statements, critical paths, buffers and pilots, are built to keep many people aligned over a long piece of work.

**Where this book departs:** a team plan is a document for coordinating people, and changing it is expensive. Your plan is often a few lines, for yourself or for an AI tool, and changing it costs almost nothing. So its purpose shifts. It is less about predicting the work and more about deciding in advance what you will check, and when. A plan you can change in a minute still earns its keep if it tells you when something has gone off track.



## What to do differently

Before you start the action you chose in [Use of Data and Information](#b1), write a five-line plan:

1. **In and out:** what this includes, and what it doesn't.
2. **Success:** what a good result looks like, plus one guardrail.
3. **Resources:** what you need, marking anything you don't have yet.
4. **Estimate:** a range, with one visible buffer.
5. **First small test:** the smallest version you can try first, and what it will tell you.

If line 3 has gaps, close them before you start. If line 5 is empty, you are about to bet everything on the first try.
