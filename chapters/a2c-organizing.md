# Organize

**Principle: the order you give data decides what you can see in it. Choose the order from your question, not from habit or from the order the data arrived in.**



## The same data, three pictures

Take a month of sales records. Arrange them by date and you see a trend: rising, falling, a dip on certain days. Arrange the same records by product and you see the mix: what sells and what doesn't. Arrange them by shop and you see where.

Nothing about the data changed. Only the order did, and each order answers a different question and hides the others. Data usually arrives in the order it was recorded, which is the order that was convenient for whoever recorded it. That is rarely the order your question needs.

To *organize* is to choose which order, so that what you're looking for becomes visible and easy to compare.



## Five ways to arrange anything

Information architecture offers a short answer to "how could I arrange this?" Richard Saul Wurman's **LATCH** framework holds that information can be organized in only five ways:

* **Location:** where it is or happened.
* **Alphabet:** by name, useful when people look things up.
* **Time:** when it happened.
* **Category:** what kind of thing it is.
* **Hierarchy:** by rank or size, from largest to smallest or most to least important.

The question types from [Information vs Data](#a1) point to an order. A **trend** question needs time. A **comparison** or **segmentation** question needs category. An **anomaly** question needs hierarchy, sorting by size so the unusual values rise to the top. A **lookup** question needs alphabet or location, so you can go straight to the item. Choosing the wrong axis buries the answer even when the data is right.



## When one order is not enough

Some data needs to be seen more than one way. Information architecture has two ideas for this:

* **Facets:** instead of filing each item in one folder, tag it with several attributes, such as date, topic and source, so you can filter by any combination.
* **Many parents:** one item can belong to more than one group. A tax form belongs under both finance and HR. Forcing it into one means someone looks in the other place and doesn't find it.

Both come from the same insight: a single folder structure answers only the question it was built for.



## Organize by what you will do with it

There is one more axis that matters for decisions: **actionability**. A common note-taking approach, the PARA method, groups notes not by subject but by use: active projects, ongoing areas of responsibility, reference resources, and archives. The same idea works for any data you have collected. Group it by what it is *for*: what bears on this decision, what you may need later, and what you can set aside.

This is the question from [Information vs Data](#a1) at work again. It decided what to collect and what to cut. Now it decides what goes on top.



## The bridge: information architecture

*Information architecture* is the discipline of organizing information so people can find and understand it. Its tools are taxonomies (categories within categories), facets and the LATCH axes.

**Where this book departs:** information architecture usually builds one lasting structure, such as a website's menu or a company's document library, that many people will use for years. It has to choose one main order and live with it. You are usually organizing for one question, once. That makes reorganizing cheap. Instead of committing to one order up front, try two or three and see which one shows you something. The best order is the one that makes the answer obvious, and you often can't know which that is until you try.



## What to do differently

Take the data you checked in [Process](#a2b).

1. Name your question type from [Information vs Data](#a1), and arrange the data on the matching axis: time, category, hierarchy, alphabet or location.
2. Re-sort it once on a different axis. Write down anything you see now that you didn't see before.
3. Split what you have into three groups: **for this decision**, **maybe later** and **set aside**. Work only from the first.

If the second sort showed you nothing new, your first order was probably right. If it did, keep both views and take them into [Interpret](#a2d).
