# Intro: Project Management Thinking

**Principle: knowing is not doing. Once you have the right information, plan how you will use it: imagine how it could fail, find what will hold it up, watch for early warnings, and adjust as reality pushes back. Project managers work this way because a plan that meets reality unchanged is rare.**



## Why project management is the bridge

*Project management* is the discipline that answers *how and when will we deliver it?* It deals with execution: sequence, time, cost and risk. It is the bridge for Section B, which is about doing the thing right by using the information right.

[Section A](#a0) ended with information structured for a decision. That is where most advice stops, and where a different kind of failure begins: the right information, used at the wrong time, in the wrong order, or not at all. There is a gap between knowing and doing, and project management is the discipline built to cross it.

AI has changed the shape of this work too. When a tool does the building, you are coordinating it: deciding the order of steps, checking what comes back, and noticing when things are drifting. That is a project manager's job, even when the project is one person and an afternoon.



## Four habits worth borrowing

**1. Imagine it has already failed.** A *pre-mortem* starts by assuming the project has failed some months from now, and works backward to list every reason why. It surfaces risks before the work begins, when they are still cheap to deal with. Asking "what could go wrong?" invites optimism. Asking "why did it go wrong?" invites honesty.

**2. Find the one thing holding everything up.** Work moves only as fast as its slowest step. *Bottleneck thinking*, from the theory of constraints, says to stop trying to speed up everything and clear that one step instead. *Critical path* thinking finds the longest chain of steps that depend on each other. A delay anywhere on that chain delays the whole result.

**3. Watch leading indicators, not only lagging ones.** A *lagging indicator* tells you something has already gone wrong: a missed deadline. A *leading indicator* warns you while you can still act: work waiting longer than usual for review, or blockers that are piling up. Project managers watch the leading ones so they hear about trouble weeks early instead of on the day.

**4. Compare the plan with what actually happened.** At the end of each stage, project managers set their estimates against the actual results and use the difference to correct the next plan. A related habit is to forecast in ranges rather than single dates, such as "likely done between Tuesday and Friday", because a single date hides how uncertain it is.



## Where this book departs

Project management is written for teams delivering large pieces of work over months. This book applies its habits to a smaller and more frequent project: **using information to get one thing done, often with an AI tool doing much of the work.**

* The project is often one person, and it lasts hours rather than months.
* The team member whose work you check is often an AI tool.
* The cycle is short. Agile methods, which since the 2001 Agile Manifesto have moved software teams from long, fixed plans to short repeated cycles, work in short, fixed cycles called sprints. With AI, one cycle of ask, check and adjust can take minutes.

One classic habit bends here. In a team, work counts as *done* when someone has reviewed it against what was agreed. AI output looks finished the moment it appears, whether it is right or not. So decide what *done* means before you start, as [Product Management Thinking](#a0) asked you to decide what a good answer looks like. Otherwise anything that looks complete will pass.



## What Section B covers

* **[Use of data and information](#b1):** turning information you know into something you do.
* **[Plan and prepare](#b2):** defining what success looks like, and starting with a small, low-risk test instead of a full rollout.
* **[Execute and monitor](#b3):** acting on the plan, and watching early-warning indicators while the work is under way.
* **[Review and reflect](#b4):** comparing what happened with what you planned, and noting what you still don't know.
* **[Adapt and iterate](#b5):** changing your approach where reality differed from the plan, and running the next cycle.



## What to do differently

Take the decision you structured in [Structure](#a2e), or any task you are about to start. Before you begin, write four lines:

1. **Pre-mortem:** it is two weeks from now and this has failed. What are the three most likely reasons?
2. **Bottleneck:** which single step is most likely to hold everything up?
3. **Early warning:** what is the first sign you would see if it were going wrong, and when will you look for it?
4. **Done:** how will you know it is finished and right, not just finished?

If the pre-mortem was easy to write, act on it now. Those risks are already known.
