

Glad you took the red pill. Welcome to the Informational Matrix.

**Principle: AI has made building cheap. What it has not made cheap is knowing what to build, which information to feed it, and how to use what comes back. That is a thinking skill, and this book trains it.**



## Why this book

Before AI, building a digital system meant months spent learning the syntax of a programming language. Today you can describe what you want to a tool like Copilot or Claude and get working code back. The bottleneck has moved.

It has moved to the person giving the instructions. To get good results from AI you need to frame the problem, ask the right question, choose the right raw material, and recognise which data is relevant and which is noise. Garbage context in, confident garbage out.

This book calls that skill *informational thinking*: the habit of turning data into information you can act on. It has three parts:

* **An eye for detail:** seeing the relevant data in front of you, including what others walk past.
* **Sense-making:** turning that data into insight. *Data* is recorded facts; it becomes *information* when it is placed in a context that answers a question. (Full definitions are in the [Glossary](#glossary).)
* **Creativity:** using that information to build a solution that works.



## Borrow, don't reinvent

When IT went mainstream, it did not invent its methods from scratch. It borrowed from older industries such as manufacturing and construction, then corrected those methods once it saw where software was different:

* Toyota's lean manufacturing gave software Kanban and Kaizen, the habits of visible flow and continuous improvement.
* The Agile Manifesto (2001) moved development away from rigid waterfall cycles toward iterative releases.
* As products grew complex, companies defined specialised product roles. Google started its Associate Product Manager program in 2002, and Lean Startup practice, user research and product analytics became standard parts of the job.

AI is at that same point now. We do not need to reinvent the wheel. We can borrow the mature practices of the fields that already handle information for a living: **information architecture, product management, project management, software engineering and data engineering.** The foundations hold. A few classic habits have to bend to fit how AI behaves, and each chapter says where.



## How the book is built

The book follows the two questions every piece of work has to answer.

| | Section A: Do the right thing | Section B: Do the thing right |
|---|---|---|
| **Bridge discipline** | Product management | Project management |
| **Core question** | What should we build, and why? | How and when will we deliver it? |
| **Success looks like** | Adoption, retention, business return | On time, on budget, on scope |
| **Where AI shifts it** | Designing probabilistic experiences and evaluations | Automating scheduling, risk forecasting and status tracking |
| **Informational task** | Choose the right information | Use the information right |

Order matters. Section A comes first because, as Russell Ackoff warned, "The righter we do the wrong thing, the wronger we become." Section A covers the steps from data to information: collect, process, organize, interpret and structure. Section B covers putting that information to work: plan, execute and monitor, review, and adapt.



## Who it is for

You do not need a technical background. People in technology already have product and project management concepts to stand on; most people outside it do not. This book explains those concepts in plain language with everyday examples, so anyone working with AI can use them.

If you are technical, you will recognise the frameworks. What will be new is using them as a way to think about information, rather than as a way to run a team.



## How to read it

The book is the spine: short chapters, principles only. Each blog post takes one chapter and applies it to a real situation. Read a chapter, then look for its posts when you want to see the principle at work.

**Before you start:** pick one task you plan to hand to AI this week. Write down the question it should answer and the information you would give it. Keep that note. By the end of Section A, you should be able to tell which parts of it were data and which were information.
