


AI can do a great deal, but only with the information you give it. Someone has to find the relevant data, give it the right context and shape it into information. That is the part AI cannot do for you.

This site teaches that skill. It calls it *informational thinking*, and builds it the way you build a muscle: one habit at a time.



## The gap it closes

There is more information at our fingertips than ever, yet getting the *right* information takes skill. The American Library Association calls this information literacy: the ability to "recognize when information is needed and have the ability to locate, evaluate, and use effectively the needed information."

People in technology have a platform to stand on here. Product management, project management, information architecture and software engineering are all, at heart, disciplines for handling information. Most people outside technology never meet these ideas. This book brings them out of the IT department and into plain language.



## What you will practise

* **See more.** Observing is not the same as seeing. You see everything on your way to work, but you rarely look for anything specific or note it to use later. Observation is paying attention on purpose so you collect specific information. That is the eye for detail.
* **Make sense of it.** Turn what you collected into information that answers a question, and get to an insight you can act on.
* **Use it.** Apply that information to build something that works: a decision, a plan, a prompt, a product.

Along the way you will practise the habits the bridge disciplines rely on. Take a data-driven approach, follow a structured process, surface your assumptions, consider alternatives, and then commit.



## How the site is organised

* **The book** is the spine: short chapters, principles only. Section A, drawing on product management, is about choosing the right information. Section B, drawing on project management, is about using it right.
* **The blog** takes one chapter at a time and applies it to an everyday situation.

The book draws on the fields I have worked in: product and project management, information architecture, user interface and visualisation, user experience, and software engineering.
