# Adapt and Iterate

**Principle: change course on evidence, not on mood. Adjust where reality proved your assumptions wrong, keep what worked, and start the next cycle from what you learned. Each round should begin better informed than the last.**



## Reality will differ from the plan

Action is rarely a straight line. However good the information and the plan, real conditions push back: a supplier is late, a guest count changes, an assumption turns out to be false. Expect it. The skill is not avoiding friction but adjusting to it quickly when it arrives.

Adapting is different from reacting. Reacting changes course because something *feels* wrong. Adapting changes course because the evidence from [Execute and Monitor](#b3) and the cause you traced in [Review and Reflect](#b4) say something specific is wrong.



## Decide what to change

The review told you where the difference came from. That decides where you go back to:

* **If the information was wrong,** return to Section A, to the step that broke. You may need a better question ([Information vs Data](#a1)), different data ([Collect](#a2a)) or a second look at the pattern ([Interpret](#a2d)).
* **If the information was used wrong,** stay in Section B. Change the actions, the plan or what you monitor.

Then choose one of three moves:

* **Keep going** if the evidence says the approach works and only needs time.
* **Adjust** one thing if a specific assumption failed. Changing one thing at a time lets you see what the change did. Changing five at once leaves you guessing again.
* **Stop** if the success you defined in [Plan and Prepare](#b2) is no longer worth reaching, or no longer reachable.



## Keep what worked

Adapting isn't only about fixing what went wrong. What worked should become your default, so that you don't have to work it out again. Correct your next estimate by how far off the last one was. Reuse the check that caught a problem early. Keep the supplier who came through.

In [the restaurant](#metaphor), the cycle starts again whenever the information changes or a new event comes along. The kitchen that learned from the last event doesn't start from scratch. It starts from its notes.



## The bridge: Agile and continuous improvement

Software teams once worked in long, fixed sequences, planning everything up front and delivering at the end. The 2001 Agile Manifesto moved them toward short, repeated cycles, each one delivering something and each one learning from the last. Toyota's lean manufacturing introduced *Kaizen*: continuous improvement through small, steady changes rather than occasional big ones.

**Where this book departs:** Agile teams iterate on a fixed rhythm, such as a sprint every few weeks. Your cycles are usually triggered by events instead: new information, a changed need, a result that missed. With an AI tool, a cycle can take minutes, which brings a new risk: iterating without learning. Asking, tweaking and asking again can feel like progress while repeating the same mistake. A cycle counts only if it starts from a lesson. And it needs an end. When the success you defined is met, stop iterating, however tempting one more improvement is.



## The loop

This is where the book closes, and where the next cycle starts. Every round of adapting ends at a question: given what I now know, what do I need to decide next? That is where [Information vs Data](#a1) began.

Doing the right thing and doing the thing right are not two stages you pass through once. They form a loop. Each time around, you choose information better because you have used it, and use it better because you chose it well.



## What to do differently

Take the lesson you wrote at the end of [Review and Reflect](#b4) and write five lines:

1. **Keep:** what worked, and will become your default.
2. **Change:** the one thing you will do differently, and the evidence for it.
3. **Go back to:** the step in Section A or Section B where that change belongs.
4. **Trigger:** what new information or need would start the next cycle.
5. **Stop:** how you will know you are done.

If line 2 has no evidence, you are reacting, not adapting. Go back to the review.
