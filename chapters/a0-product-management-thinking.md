# Intro: Product Management Thinking

**Principle: before you gather any information, decide what result you want and state your bet about how to get it. Product managers work this way because building the wrong thing well is the most expensive mistake there is. With AI, everyone who asks for something is now doing a product manager's job.**



## Why product management is the bridge

*Product management* is the discipline that answers *what should we build, and why?* It is the bridge for Section A, which is about doing the right thing by choosing the right information.

Product managers rarely build anything themselves. Their job is to decide what is worth building, and to justify that decision with evidence. Richard Rumelt frames strategy the same way, as problem-solving: what are the specific challenges we should focus on, and how should we address them?

AI has put many more people in that position. You describe what you want and something else builds it. The building is fast and cheap. Deciding what to ask for, and which information to give it, is not.



## Four habits worth borrowing

Product management has many tools. Four habits matter most for choosing information.

**1. Aim at the outcome, not the output.** An *output* is what you make: a feature, a report, a prompt. An *outcome* is what actually happens after you act on a decision: here, what changes because of what you made. Product managers watch outcome measures, such as whether people keep using a product, rather than vanity numbers, such as total downloads, which look good and decide nothing. An *opportunity solution tree* starts from one desired outcome at the top and works down to the opportunities, solutions and small experiments beneath it. That keeps attention on the problem instead of on whatever is easiest to make.

**2. State your bet before you look.** *Hypothesis-driven thinking* frames each decision as a testable guess before anyone opens the data: "If we change [X] for [user Y], we expect [measure Z] to move, because [reason]." The sentence tells you which data matters. It also tells you, afterward, whether you were right.

**3. Pair the what with the why.** Numbers show *what* is happening. Talking to people, watching them, and reading their complaints shows *why*. Product managers combine the two, called *triangulation*, because a number on its own is easy to misread. Averages hide the most; the extreme cases, your heaviest users and the people who gave up, usually show more.

**4. Look past the first effect.** *Second-order thinking* asks what happens after the immediate result. Aggressive pop-ups can raise clicks today and lower how many people stay in six months. So product managers pair each measure they want to grow with a *guardrail*, a second measure that warns when the gain is costing something else.



## Where this book departs

Product management is written for teams building products for customers. This book applies the same habits to a smaller and more frequent product: **the information you decide with, and the context you hand to AI.**

* The user is often you, at the moment you have to decide something.
* The "build" is often a single answer, document or piece of code from an AI tool, produced in minutes rather than months.
* Because building is cheap, the bet comes cheap too. You can test a hypothesis by asking, looking at the result, and asking again. What doesn't get cheaper is the judgment about what to ask, which is why it now matters more.

One classic habit bends here. In product management, evaluating a result usually means waiting for real users. AI results vary from one run to the next, so you need to decide *before* you ask what a good answer would look like. Otherwise any plausible answer will seem good enough.

## What to do differently

Take the task you picked in the Preface, or any request you are about to give an AI tool. Before you write the request, write one sentence:

> If I get [this result], I expect [this outcome] to change, because [this reason]. I will know it worked when [this sign].

If you can't fill in the outcome, you are about to ask for an output nobody needs. If you can't fill in the sign, decide what a good answer looks like before you ask.
