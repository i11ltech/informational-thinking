# Steps to Information

**Principle: data does not turn into information in one leap. It passes through five steps: collect, process, organize, interpret and structure. Each step answers a different question and fails in a different way, and the question you started from steers all five.**



## Why break it into steps

I once went into my backyard after a long time away and found it a mess. The temptation was to think about the whole mess at once. What worked was starting small and clearing one piece at a time.

Data feels the same. A spreadsheet, an inbox or a folder of notes can look like one overwhelming problem. It becomes manageable when you split the work into steps and ask only one thing of each.

Splitting the work also shows you where it went wrong. If the final answer is poor, you can ask at which step it broke: did you gather the wrong data, fail to check it, arrange it badly, misread it, or present it so nobody could use it? Each of those has a different fix.



## The five steps

| Step | The question it answers | What it trains | It goes wrong when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[Collect](#a2a)** | What data do I need, and where is it? | An eye for detail: observing on purpose | You gather what is easy to reach instead of what the question needs |
| **[Process](#a2b)** | Can I trust this data, and can I compare it? | Judgment: checking and cleaning | You analyse data you never checked |
| **[Organize](#a2c)** | How should I arrange it so I can find and compare it? | Order: choosing how to group things | You group it in a way that hides what you were looking for |
| **[Interpret](#a2d)** | What pattern, trend or relationship does it show? | Sense-making | You see what you expected instead of what is there |
| **[Structure](#a2e)** | How do I present it so someone can act on it? | Clarity: saying it concisely and accessibly | The answer is right but buried |

**Collect** is where informational thinking starts: noticing which data exists and which of it bears on your question. That part is the *signal*; the rest is *noise*. Observing is not the same as seeing. You see everything on your way to work, but you rarely look for anything specific.

**Process** is the step most people skip. Before you trust a number, check whether it is accurate, current and from a source you can rely on, and put values into a form you can compare.

**Organize** gives the data an order. Information architecture holds that there are only a few basic ways to arrange anything, such as by time, by category or by rank. The order you pick decides what you can see.

**Interpret** is where data becomes information: you find the pattern and connect it to the question. Adding your *knowledge*, what you bring from experience and expertise, at this step is what turns an observation into an insight.

**Structure** is where information reaches the person who decides, whether that is someone else, an AI tool or you next week. The most important point goes first.



## The bridge: information literacy

Information literacy, as the American Library Association defines it, is the ability to "recognize when information is needed and have the ability to locate, evaluate, and use effectively the needed information." Most accounts of it list a sequence: identify the need, find, evaluate, analyse, apply.

The five steps here follow the same arc. Identifying the need is the question from [Information vs Data](#a1). Finding maps to collect, evaluating to process, and analysing to interpret.

**Where this book departs:** information literacy is written for someone searching for information that already exists, such as a book, an article or a report. This book is about *making* information out of raw data, often data nobody has looked at yet. That adds two steps information literacy takes for granted. **Organize** comes from information architecture, because raw data has no order until you give it one. **Structure** comes from product management and visualisation, because information you can't hand to someone in a usable form still decides nothing.



## The steps are not a strict line

You will loop back. Interpreting often shows that you collected the wrong thing. Structuring for a reader often shows that your organization doesn't fit their question. Going back is the method working, not failing.

What doesn't change is the starting point. Each step is judged by the same test from [Information vs Data](#a1): does this help answer the question behind the decision?



## What to do differently

Take the decision and question you wrote at the end of [Information vs Data](#a1). Before you start, write one line for each step:

1. **Collect:** what data will I use, and where will it come from?
2. **Process:** how will I check that it is accurate and current?
3. **Organize:** how will I arrange it: by time, category, rank or something else?
4. **Interpret:** what pattern would change my decision?
5. **Structure:** what is the one sentence I would put first?

Look for the line you couldn't fill in or were tempted to skip. That is the step where your answer is most likely to fail. The chapters that follow take one step each.
