# Interpret

**Principle: a pattern is not yet an insight. Interpreting means finding the pattern, testing whether it is real and what causes it, and connecting it to your decision. The biggest risk at this step is seeing what you expected to see.**



## Three moves

This is the step where data becomes information: where the patterns, trends and relationships in the data are turned into something you can act on. It has three moves, and most mistakes come from stopping after the first.

1. **Notice** a pattern: a change, a difference between groups, something unusual.
2. **Test** it: is it real, and what is causing it?
3. **Connect** it: what does it mean for the decision in front of you?

The question type from [Information vs Data](#a1) tells you what kind of pattern to look for. The order you chose in [Organize](#a2c) is what makes it visible. The rest of this chapter is about the two moves after noticing.



## Test: is it real?

A pattern you have just noticed is a guess. Before you lean on it, ask:

* **Could something outside explain it?** Seasons, holidays, a campaign or an outage can move a number with no help from the thing you are studying.
* **Does moving together mean causing?** Two numbers rising at the same time may share a cause, or may be coincidence. One driving the other is only one of the possibilities.
* **Does the total hide opposite moves?** An overall figure can stay flat while one group drops sharply and another rises to cover it. Split the total by group before trusting it.
* **Does the why agree with the what?** As [Product Management Thinking](#a0) argued, pair the number with what people say and do. If the reasons don't fit the pattern, one of them is wrong.
* **Have you asked why more than once?** The first answer to "why did this happen?" is usually a symptom. Ask again of that answer, and again, until you reach something you could act on. Product managers call this the *five whys*.



## Connect: so what?

A tested pattern still isn't information until it answers your question. Ask: *so what does this mean for my decision?* If the answer is "nothing", the pattern is interesting but it is *noise* for this question, however striking it is.

This is also where *knowledge* comes in: what you bring from experience and expertise. Information literacy describes analysing as comparing new information with what you already know. Does this fit how you understood things, or contradict it? A pattern that contradicts your experience deserves a second test, not an automatic dismissal. Sometimes the experience is out of date.



## Seeing what you expected

[Product Management Thinking](#a0) asked you to state your bet before you look. That bet tells you which data matters. It also makes you want to be right.

*Confirmation bias* is the habit of noticing evidence that supports what you already believe and passing over evidence that doesn't. The defence is to look on purpose for the data that would prove your bet wrong, and to treat finding it as success rather than failure.



## The bridge: diagnosing causes in product analytics

Product managers don't stop at a dashboard. They break a top-line number into its parts to find the one that moved. They check for outside factors before crediting their own changes. They slice totals by group, and they run controlled experiments, such as A/B tests, to prove that a change caused a result.

**Where this book departs:** experiments need time, traffic and a product to test on. For most of your decisions you can't run one. You get one look at the data and then you have to decide. So instead of proof, aim for honesty about confidence: say what you think the cause is, which alternatives you checked, and what would change your mind.

The same applies when an AI tool does the interpreting. It will readily produce a pattern and a convincing explanation for it. The three moves still apply, and the testing is still yours to do.



## What to do differently

When you think you have found something, write it out in five parts before you act on it:

> The data shows **[pattern]**. I think it is because **[cause]**. I ruled out **[alternative]**. For my decision this means **[implication]**. I would change my mind if **[evidence]**.

If you can't fill in the alternative you ruled out, you haven't tested the pattern. If you can't fill in the implication, it isn't information yet. Take the finished sentence into [Structure](#a2e).
