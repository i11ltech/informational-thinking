# Glossary

The book uses these terms in one sense only. When a chapter says *information*, it means exactly what is written here.



## The core chain

These terms follow the order of the book's argument: from raw data, through a decision, to what happens in the world.

**Data:** Recorded facts and figures, such as numbers, events and observations, taken on their own. A list of daily sales figures is data. Data has potential, but answers nothing yet. See [Information vs Data](#a1).

**Information:** Data placed in context so it answers a specific question and can support a decision. The same data can be information to one person and noise to another; the question decides which.

**Knowledge:** What you bring from experience and expertise. Added to information, it supports a stronger decision than either gives alone.

**Signal:** The part of the data that bears on the question you are answering. Signal is relative: what is signal for one question is noise for another.

**Noise:** Data that does not bear on the question in front of you. It is not wrong or useless in itself; it is beside the point for now.

**Decision:** A choice between options that commits you to an action. Every information need starts from one. If you can't name the decision, you don't need information yet.

**Outcome:** What actually happens after you act on a decision. The outcome is how you judge whether you chose the right information and used it right.



## Thinking and literacy

**Informational thinking:** The habit of turning data into information you can act on. It has three parts: an eye for detail (seeing the relevant data), sense-making (turning it into insight), and creativity (using it to build a solution that works).

**Information need:** A gap in understanding that you try to fill with facts, data or ideas. Needs arise when you want to satisfy curiosity, make a decision, complete a task or solve a problem.

**Information literacy:** In the American Library Association's definition, the ability to "recognize when information is needed and have the ability to locate, evaluate, and use effectively the needed information."

**Data as a product:** Treating data as something designed for a user and a purpose, rather than as an asset to collect and hoard. This book applies the idea to your own thinking: the user is you, at the moment you have to decide.



## Bridge disciplines

The book borrows from these fields. Each chapter names which one it draws on and where it departs from it.

**Product management:** The discipline that answers *what should we build, and why?* It judges success by adoption, retention and business return. It is the bridge for Section A: doing the right thing, choosing the right information.

**Project management:** The discipline that answers *how and when will we deliver it?* It judges success by delivery on time, on budget and on scope. It is the bridge for Section B: doing the thing right, using the information right.

**Information architecture:** The practice of organizing, structuring and labelling information so people can find and use it. It is the bridge for the organize and structure steps in Section A.
