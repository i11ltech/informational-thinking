# Review and Reflect

**Principle: after you act, compare what happened with what you expected, and trace any difference back to its cause. Was the information wrong, or was it used wrong? Write the lesson down and pass it to whoever chooses the next "what".**



## Compare the outcome with the plan

An *outcome* is what actually happens after you act on a decision. It is how you judge whether the information was chosen and used right. That makes review the point where the whole book gets tested.

You already wrote down what to compare against. [Plan and Prepare](#b2) gave you a definition of success, a guardrail and an estimate. Set each one beside what actually happened:

* Did you get the result you defined as success?
* Did the guardrail hold, or did the gain cost something else?
* How did your estimate compare with the actual time and effort?

In [the restaurant](#metaphor), this is the morning after the event: checking the results against the goals, and gathering feedback.



## Gather the why, not only the what

The numbers tell you *what* happened: dishes served, budget spent, time taken. To learn *why*, ask the people involved: the client, the guests, the cooks. As [Product Management Thinking](#a0) argued, a number on its own is easy to misread. That holds for reviewing your own work too.



## Trace the difference

When the outcome differs from the plan, find where the difference came from. The two sections of this book give you two places to look:

* **The information was wrong.** The [Steps to Information](#a2) broke somewhere. You collected the convenient data, didn't check it, arranged it so the answer was hidden, read the pattern you expected, or buried the answer.
* **The information was right, but it was used wrong.** The action was unclear, the plan was missing a resource, or a warning was seen but not acted on.

The fixes are different. Wrong information needs a better question or better data next time. Wrong use needs a better plan or closer monitoring.

Check the good results as well. A good outcome can hide a lucky guess. If you can't explain *why* it worked, you can't count on it working again.



## Note what you still don't know

Every piece of work leaves questions behind: an assumption you never tested, data you couldn't get, a cause you are still guessing at. Write them down. They are the starting point for the next round of [Collect](#a2a).



## The bridge: lessons learned

Project management closes work by capturing *lessons learned*, and by comparing planned with actual effort so that the next estimate can be corrected by the difference. Some practitioners keep a reflective journal: a regular record of decisions, open questions and how their approach held up in a real situation.

**Where this book departs:** in a team, review is a meeting at the end of a project or a cycle, and its lessons go into a document. Yours is often five minutes, alone, right after the work. With an AI tool there is an extra temptation to skip it: when a result disappoints, it is easy to just ask again. Asking again without reviewing usually repeats the same mistake in different words. Five minutes of tracing the cause first makes the next attempt different, not just new.

Review also closes the loop between the two hats. In the restaurant, the lessons are shared with the people who chose the menu. In your work, the lessons about *use* often say something about the *choice*, so take them back to Section A.



## What to do differently

When you finish the work you monitored in [Execute and Monitor](#b3), take five minutes and write:

1. **Expected versus happened:** your success measure beside the result.
2. **Guardrail and estimate:** did the guardrail hold? How far off was the estimate?
3. **Cause:** was the information wrong, or was it used wrong? At which step?
4. **Lesson:** one thing you would do differently, and who else needs to hear it.
5. **Still unknown:** one question you couldn't answer this time.

Take line 4 into [Adapt and Iterate](#b5).
