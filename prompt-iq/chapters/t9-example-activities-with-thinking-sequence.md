# Example Activities with Thinking Sequence

**Principle: the hats are most useful in sequence. The central question is not "what information do I have?" but "what kind of thinking will help me understand this information and use it well?", and the answer usually changes as the work moves on.**



## Choosing your hat

| Hat | Main question | Best moment to use it |
|---|---|---|
| [Prelude: Data is in the Details](#t1) | What is actually inside the data, across different cuts? | Exploring raw numbers, metrics or anomalies |
| [Systems Thinking](#t2) | How do the parts, loops and delays interact? | Recurring bottlenecks, side effects, complex operations |
| [Analytical Thinking](#t3) | What are the components, drivers and trade-offs? | Structuring decisions, root causes, comparisons |
| [Probabilistic and Deterministic](#t4) | What is certain, and what is only likely? | Forecasts, estimates, deciding with incomplete information |
| [Critical Thinking](#t5) | Where are the flaws, biases or weak assumptions? | Reviewing plans, claims or high-stakes proposals |
| [Strategic Thinking](#t6) | Where should we aim, given what is changing? | Setting direction, allocating scarce resources |
| [Design Thinking](#t7) | What solves real friction in practice? | Products, workflows, services, experiences |
| [Paradoxical Thinking](#t8) | What false trade-off or inherited rule can we move past? | Breaking stalemates, rethinking from scratch |

Each hat reveals something different. Attention to detail establishes what is known. System thinking shows how the parts interact. Analysis organizes the problem. Probabilistic thinking separates the certain from the likely. Critical thinking tests claims. Strategic thinking directs choices. Design thinking develops and tests possibilities. Paradoxical thinking reconsiders the foundations.



## How to read the activities

Each activity is a practice exercise, not a real case. It shows one sensible order of hats for a common kind of task, with the question each hat asks and a line you could put in a prompt. The order is a suggestion. Change it when the problem demands it.



## Activity 1: Delivery delays rose last month

*You need to decide whether to change the shipping process.*

| Step | Hat | Question | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Details | What exactly rose, and for whom? | "Identify the three most useful data points to review before deciding whether to change the shipping process." |
| 2 | System | What else changed that could feed the delays? | "Map the handoffs between orders, warehouse and carrier, and any delay between a change and its effect." |
| 3 | Analytical | Which driver explains most of the rise? | "For each possible cause, say whether the evidence shows correlation or causation." |
| 4 | Critical | What are we assuming? | "List the assumptions behind the leading explanation, and the strongest case against it." |
| 5 | Probabilistic | How sure are we? | "Separate confirmed facts from estimates, and flag conclusions that depend on unverified assumptions." |

Notice what comes last: the decision. The sequence ends with how confident you are, so the decision is made knowing its risks.



## Activity 2: Customers abandon a sign-up form

*You want more people to finish signing up.*

| Step | Hat | Question | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Details | Where exactly do people stop? | "Before suggesting fixes, list the data needed to find the step where most people leave." |
| 2 | Design | What need sits underneath? | "Give five reasons a person might stop partway through, from their point of view. Then reframe the problem as a human need." |
| 3 | Paradoxical | Is there a false trade-off? | "We need enough information for verification and as few fields as possible. Don't choose between them; propose a design that serves both." |
| 4 | Critical | Which idea could backfire? | "For each proposed change, say how it could make things worse." |
| 5 | Strategic | What do we test first? | "Pick one change we can test in a week, and say what result would tell us to continue." |



## Activity 3: Choosing between two launch dates

*Engineering wants more time. Sales wants the earlier date.*

| Step | Hat | Question | Prompt line |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Details | What does each date actually depend on? | "List what must be true for each date: engineering readiness, support coverage and customer commitments." |
| 2 | Strategic | What matters more over the next year? | "Compare both dates by long-term customer impact, not only this quarter's targets." |
| 3 | Probabilistic | How likely is each date to hold? | "For each date, estimate how likely it is to slip, and what it depends on." |
| 4 | Paradoxical | Is there a third option? | "Suggest a way to meet the sales commitment without shipping unfinished work, such as a limited early release." |
| 5 | Critical | What would we regret? | "Imagine the chosen date has gone badly. What are the three most likely reasons?" |



## The bridge

The sequences follow the shape of the main book. Details first, as in [Collect](book.html#a2a) and [Process](book.html#a2b). Sense-making next, as in [Interpret](book.html#a2d). Then a decision you can act on and check, as in [Plan and Prepare](book.html#b2) and [Review and Reflect](book.html#b4).

**Where this departs:** a team would spread these steps across meetings. With a model, you can run a whole sequence in one sitting, one prompt per hat, reading each answer before writing the next. Ask for each step separately rather than all at once. Each answer should be checked before the next one builds on it, as [Execute and Monitor](book.html#b3) advised.



## What to do differently

Take a decision you are facing this week.

1. Pick three or four hats from the table, in the order that fits.
2. Write one prompt line for each.
3. Run them one at a time, and note where an answer changed which hat you needed next.

That last note is the skill: knowing when to switch.
