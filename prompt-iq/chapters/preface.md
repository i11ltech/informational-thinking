# Preface

**Principle: AI is only as smart as the context you give it. This is not a book of prompt tricks. It is about staying in the driver's seat: knowing your situation in detail, and putting that detail into what you ask.**



## Not another prompt engineering book

There are plenty of lists of prompt techniques. Many of them are useful, and some appear in this book. But AI tools can now write prompts on their own, and the catalogue of prompt types keeps changing. A book that only taught templates would date quickly.

What doesn't date is the thinking behind a good prompt. Before you can tell an AI tool what you need, you have to know it yourself: the situation, the goal, the constraints, what a good answer would look like. That is the subject of this book.



## Stay in the loop

It is tempting to let AI do the prompting as well as the answering. Modern tools are good at filling gaps, and leaving details out often still gets you something that looks fine.

The risk is not that the AI does a bad job. The risk is that you stop knowing what it was asked to do. When you don't write the detail, you don't think through the detail, and you can't tell whether the answer fits your situation or only sounds as if it does.

So this book asks for a habit of **extreme ownership**: you decide what goes into the prompt, you know why each part is there, and you check what comes back. The more detail you write, the more you understand your own problem, and the better the context you give the AI. Writing the prompt is part of the thinking, not a chore before it.



## Your Prompt IQ

This book is a companion to [Informational Thinking](book.html#preface). That book is about turning data into information you can act on. This one applies it to a single, everyday piece of information: the prompt.

Both books use the idea of an *informational quotient*, or IQ, in two linked senses (see the [Glossary](book.html#glossary)):

* **A prompt's IQ** is how much of your real situation it carries. A prompt anyone could send unchanged has a low IQ. A prompt that only makes sense for your situation has a high one.
* **Your IQ** is how well you find the relevant information, make sense of it and use it.

Raising the first is how you exercise the second.



## How this book is built

* **[Part I: The Foundations](#f1):** what makes a prompt informative, its parts, and the difference between commanding a model and steering it.
* **Part II: Informational Prompt:** each part of a prompt in turn: context, audience and purpose, constraints, examples and format.
* **Part III: Types of Prompts:** common techniques, seen as different ways of giving the model information.
* **Part IV: Beyond Prompt Engineering:** from writing one prompt to shaping everything the model knows.



## Before you start

Find the last prompt you sent to an AI tool that gave you a disappointing answer. Keep it. By the end of Part I you should be able to say what information was missing from it.
