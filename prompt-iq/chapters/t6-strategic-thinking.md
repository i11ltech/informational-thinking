# Strategic Thinking

**Principle: strategy connects today's choices to a longer-term aim. It asks not just "can we do this?" but "should we do this, here and now, given how things are changing?" And it means choosing what not to do, not only listing what you would like to do.**



## What this hat asks

**Where should we aim, given what is changing around us?**

Strategic thinking helps decide what matters most, where to put limited time and resources, and how present actions shape future opportunities and risks. Wear it when setting direction, choosing priorities, or deciding between options that lead to different futures.

Richard Rumelt frames strategy as problem-solving: what are the specific challenges we should focus on, and how should we address them? A strategy that names no challenge is a list of ambitions.



## Three habits

* **Say what you will not do.** Real strategy requires trade-offs and focus. Every "yes" implies a "no"; the cost of the options you give up is the *opportunity cost* of the one you choose.
* **Anticipate second-order moves.** Consider how others, such as customers, partners, competitors or regulators, will react once you act.
* **Build in room to adapt.** Order your commitments so you learn early, and keep options open while the situation is uncertain.



## In a prompt

Ask a model for a strategy without constraints and you will get a wish list: every reasonable initiative, nicely formatted. Strategic prompts include the trade-off:

> We are a team of four with one quarter. Our goal is to reduce the number of customers who cancel in their first month. Suggest no more than two initiatives. For each, say what we would have to stop doing to make room for it, how customers might react, and what we would learn in the first three weeks that would tell us whether to continue.

The prompt names a limit (four people, one quarter), forces focus (no more than two), asks for the "no" (what to stop), the second-order effect (how customers might react), and the early learning (the first three weeks).

When you read the answer, check that the trade-offs are real. "Stop doing low-value work" is not a trade-off. "Pause the newsletter for a quarter" is.



## The bridge

This hat draws on [Product Management Thinking](book.html#a0): aim at the outcome rather than the output, state your bet before you look, and think past the first effect. Building in room to adapt is [Plan and Prepare](book.html#b2)'s advice to start with a small test, and [Adapt and Iterate](book.html#b5)'s loop.

**Where this departs:** strategy is usually written for an organisation over years. In a prompt, it is often a single decision over weeks. The habits are the same, at a smaller scale: choose, give something up, and learn early.



## What to do differently

Before you ask a model for a plan or a recommendation, write three lines and put them in the prompt:

1. **The aim:** the outcome you want, and by when.
2. **The limit:** the time, people or money you actually have.
3. **The "no":** one thing you are willing to stop or delay.

If you can't write line 3, you are asking for a wish list, not a strategy.
